# Probes for conceptual content

Applies to theories, frameworks, typologies and models in management, economics without formal apparatus, social sciences and similar fields. Here causality is rarely a single equation. Understanding means being able to state four things: the mechanism (through which pathway one thing leads to another), the boundary conditions (when it holds and when not), the counterfactual (what would happen without it), and competing explanations (which other theory explains the same observation).

A common failure in these subjects is that learners can reproduce the framework (the four boxes, the five forces, the list of factors) without knowing why the boxes are what they are and how they connect. Probe below the surface of the framework.

Pick the probes that fit. One well-chosen probe beats a checklist.

## Purpose and core claim

- "What problem does this theory (framework) solve? What question would someone have had before it existed?"
- "State the core claim in one sentence, as a causal statement: what leads to what?"
- "What would a manager (actor) do differently if they knew this theory than if they did not?"

## Mechanism

- "Through which mechanism does X lead to Y? What happens step by step in between?"
- "Which actors are involved, and what do they want? Why do they behave this way?"
- If the learner gives a label instead of a mechanism ("because of synergies", "due to market power"), ask what exactly happens: "What do the synergies consist of concretely? Who saves what, and why?"

## Boundary conditions

- "Under which conditions does this not hold?"
- "In which industry (company size, culture, time period) would you expect the effect to be weaker or reversed? Why?"
- "Which assumption about the actors (rationality, information, goals) does the theory need? What happens if it fails?"

## Counterfactual and causality

- "What would happen without X? How do you know that X is the cause and not just a companion?"
- "Could the causality also run the other way?"
- "Which third factor could explain both X and Y?"
- "What observation would show that the theory is wrong in a given case?"

## Competing explanations

- "Which other theory explains the same phenomenon? Where do the two make different predictions?"
- "How does this framework differ from [similar framework]? When is which one the better tool?"
- Present two cases that look similar but where the concept applies to only one (contrasting cases), and ask which one and why. Give the explanation of the underlying principle only after the learner has compared.

## Levels and trade-offs

- "At which level does this argument operate: individual, team, organization, market? What changes if you move one level up or down?"
- "What does the recommended course of action cost? What trade-off does it imply?"

## Application

- "Apply it to [a company or situation the learner knows]. What does the theory predict there? Does that fit what actually happened?"
- If the learner's application only relabels facts with the framework's terms, ask what the framework adds: "What do you know now that you did not know before applying it?"

## Transfer in conceptual subjects

A good conceptual transfer question presents a new case with a different surface (another industry, another era, a non-business setting with the same structure) and asks the learner to diagnose it with the concept, including whether it applies at all. The strongest transfer questions contain a twist: a boundary condition is violated, so the naive application gives the wrong answer and the learner must notice.
